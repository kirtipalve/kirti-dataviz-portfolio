# The final data story

**[The Color Noise Illusion (Shorthand)](https://carnegiemellon.shorthandstories.com/color-noise-is-everywhere/index.html)**

Color noise is sold as a productivity hack, but the best available review found a small benefit only for people with ADHD or elevated attention symptoms, a slight cost for everyone else, and no brown noise studies.

This project builds on [Part I](final-project-part-one), where I proposed a personal Spotify listening story, and [Part II](final-project-part-two), where I switched to the colors of noise and focus, built a storyboard and draft charts, and tested the story with three people.

# Changes made since Part II

In Part II I showed my storyboard and draft charts to three readers. They understood the main question right away, but they were surprised that no brown noise studies existed, and they found effect sizes and the ADHD groups hard to interpret. I used that feedback to shape the final story.

| Part II feedback | What I did in the final story |
|---|---|
| Surprised that no brown noise studies existed | Made the gap its own section, with a chart placing white, pink and brown noise by social media hype and research evidence |
| Effect size was hard to interpret | Showed the effects on a labeled scale (hurts performance / helps performance), stated the two numbers (+0.25 and -0.21), and led with "the same hack can help one group and hurt another" |
| Wanted more context on the three noises | Added a card for each noise with a short description |
| Wanted to hear the sounds | Added short samples for each noise, generated in the browser |
| Wanted the gap between online claims and the evidence | Opened with the trend (TikTok view counts) and built the story toward the hype vs. evidence section |
| Wanted to know who was studied | Partly addressed: the story says the evidence comes from young people with ADHD or elevated symptoms and gives study and participant counts. I did not include the separate who-was-studied chart in the final version. |

## The audience

My primary audience is graduate students and young professionals who use background sound to study or work. A secondary audience is anyone curious whether popular productivity trends hold up. My three Part II readers were general readers without a research background, so I wrote the story for that level, with plain language and no statistics beyond the two headline effects. Their interest in the gap between popularity and evidence is what I built the second half of the story around.

## Final design decisions

- **One idea per section,** in scroll order: the trend, the surprising data, why it splits, hype vs. evidence, and what to do.
- **Two headline numbers only.** I kept the story to the two main effects and saved the details for this page.
- **Labeled the hype vs. evidence chart as qualitative,** because I did not measure social media attention for every color.
- **Generated the cover animation and audio samples in the browser,** so I needed no stock photos or recordings and avoided copyright problems.
- **Hedged my claims.** The review covered research through November 2023, was mostly lab tasks in young people, and newer brown noise work may exist.
- **Chose "run an experiment of one" as the call to action** instead of recommending or discouraging noise, because the evidence is small and mixed.
- **Checked the paper myself.** I noticed the abstract and results text give slightly different values for the non-ADHD group (-0.212 and -0.206), and that two studies are swapped between the paper's forest figure and its table, so I used the abstract and Table 2 values.

What stood out most is that the gap became the story, not a footnote. Reading the original paper instead of summaries mattered, because the numbers did not always agree. Showing drafts to real readers exposed confusion I could not see myself, especially around effect size.

# Final thoughts

I switched topics between Part I and Part II, so I had less time than I would have liked to test the final version. With more time I would show the finished story to the same readers again, add the who-was-studied visual I dropped, and check whether any brown noise studies have appeared since November 2023. The part I found most interesting was that the most popular color has the least research behind it.
