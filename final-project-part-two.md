# Wireframes / storyboards

**Topic change:** My Part I proposal was a personal Spotify listening story. For Part II I switched to the colors of noise (white, pink, brown) and focus so that it resonates with the audience more.

**One-sentence summary:** Color noise is everywhere as a focus aid, but the best review of the research found a small benefit only for people with ADHD symptoms, a slight cost for everyone else, and no studies of brown noise at all.

# User research 

## Target audience

My primary audience is students and young professionals who use background noise while studying or working. A secondary audience is people interested in productivity trends and whether popular focus techniques are supported by research.

**Approach to identifying representatives:** I planned to interview three people who regularly study or work with background audio. I looked for people who were familiar with using music, ambient sounds, or noise while concentrating, but who did not necessarily have prior knowledge of the scientific research on colored noise. This allowed me to test whether the story was understandable to someone approaching the topic as a general reader.

## Interview script

**Research Goal:** Understand whether readers can understand the main claim of my color-noise story, whether the visualizations make the research easy to interpret, and whether the story changes how they think about white, pink, and brown noise.

| Goal | Questions to Ask |
|------|------------------|
| Understand if the story is clear | What do you think this story is about? |
| Test the opening | After seeing the opening, what question do you think the story is trying to answer? |
| Assess visualization clarity | Which visualization was easiest to understand? Why? |
| Test the main finding | What do you think the research says about noise and focus? |
| Test the research gap | What do you take away from the visualization showing the number of studies for each type of noise? |
| Identify confusing elements | Was anything confusing or unclear? What would help you understand it better? |
| Test the takeaway | After seeing the whole story, what would you conclude about using noise to focus? |
| Get improvement ideas | What would make you want to keep reading? What would you change or add? |

## Interview findings

I showed the early storyboard and draft visualizations to three people who regularly study or work with background audio.

| Questions | Interview 1 (CMU student, early 20s) | Interview 2 (young professional, 20s) | Interview 3 (graduate student, 20s) |
|-----------|------------|------------|------------|
| What do you think this story is about? | "It's basically asking whether all the noise people use for studying actually helps you focus." | "It's about whether brown noise and other types of noise really work, versus it just being a productivity trend." | "I think it's about what the research actually says about using different sounds to concentrate." |
| What question do you think the story is answering? | "Whether brown noise actually makes you focus better." | "If noise helps everyone or if it only helps certain people." | "Whether there's actual scientific evidence behind all these focus-noise recommendations." |
| Which visualization was clearest? | "The comparison between the two groups was the easiest for me to understand." | "The number of studies for white, pink, and brown noise was really clear because you immediately see that brown has nothing." | "I liked the spectrum sketch because it helped me understand what makes the three types different." |
| What was confusing or unclear? | "I wasn't totally sure what the effect size meant. I understood that it was small, but I didn't know how small." | "At first I thought the three types of noise had been studied equally, so I think that could be made clearer earlier." | "I wanted a little more explanation of what the ADHD group actually means and who was included in those studies." |
| What surprised you? | "I was surprised that there weren't any brown noise studies, because brown noise seems like the one everyone talks about." | "I expected the research to show a much bigger improvement in focus." | "I didn't realize that most of the research was on children rather than adults." |
| What do you think the research says about noise and focus? | "It seems like it might help some people, but it's definitely not a universal thing." | "The effect seems pretty small, and it depends on the person." | "I wouldn't say the research proves that noise makes you more productive. It seems more limited than that." |
| What do you take away from the brown-noise research gap? | "That people are making pretty strong claims about something that hasn't really been studied." | "It makes me question whether brown noise is actually better than white or pink noise." | "I think the lack of studies is almost as interesting as the results of the studies that do exist." |
| What would make you want to keep reading? | "I'd want to hear the actual sounds while reading about them." | "Maybe explain why people think brown noise works so well in the first place." | "I'd want to see the actual size of the effect and understand what the researchers were measuring." |

## Key findings and patterns

**Consistent feedback across all interviews:**
- All three interviewees understood the main question as whether colored noise actually improves focus.
- All three were surprised by the lack of research specifically on brown noise.
- All three understood that the research does not support a simple claim that noise improves focus for everyone.
- The interviewees generally preferred the simpler comparison and study-count visualizations over more technical statistical explanations.

**Conflicting feedback:**
- Interview 1 found the group comparison most useful, while Interview 3 preferred the frequency-spectrum explanation.
- Interview 2 was most interested in the cultural/productivity trend around brown noise, while the other two were more interested in the scientific evidence.
- Interviewees differed in how much statistical explanation they wanted. Some wanted more explanation of the effect size, while others preferred a simpler takeaway.

**Critical gaps identified:**
- The meaning of the effect size was not immediately obvious to everyone.
- The distinction between white, pink, and brown noise could be explained more clearly before presenting the research results.
- The audience may assume that brown noise has been extensively researched because it is so common in online productivity content.
- The story should explain more clearly who participated in the underlying studies and what "ADHD/high-symptom" groups mean.

**Highest-value feedback:**
- The most useful feedback was that the absence of brown-noise research was itself a compelling finding. Rather than treating it as a minor limitation, I can make the research gap a central part of the story.
- The interviews also showed that readers need help interpreting the size of the effect, rather than simply being shown a statistical value.

# Identified changes for Part III

| Research synthesis | Anticipated changes for Part III |
|---------------------|----------------------------------|
| Interviewees were surprised that there were no eligible brown-noise studies | Make the 12 white, 1 pink, and 0 brown study comparison a prominent visualization |
| The effect size was difficult to interpret | Add a short plain-language explanation of what the effect size means and emphasize that the average effect is small |
| Readers wanted more context about the three types of noise | Add a short introduction explaining the differences between white, pink, and brown noise |
| Interviewees wanted to understand who was studied | Add a visual showing the populations represented in the research |
| The comparison between groups was easy to understand | Keep the ADHD/high-symptom versus comparison-group visualization as one of the central visuals |
| Interviewees were interested in the gap between online claims and scientific evidence | Strengthen the narrative contrast between the popularity of brown noise and the limited research supporting it |
| Some interviewees wanted to hear the actual sounds | Keep short audio clips for white, pink, and brown noise so readers can experience the sounds while reading |
